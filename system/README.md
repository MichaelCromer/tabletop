# System

This is a modular TTRPG system.

## Modes of Play

In any TTRPG, the game advances by moving between the different modes of play. A "mode" is our term for the combination of narrative mood, player expectations, and mechanical rulesets that apply to the gameplay at any given moment.  The GM should always be sensitive to the mode the game is currently in and shift narration, detail, and pacing to match. Controlling this flow is the most powerful tool available for setting the mood and texture of the game.

### Narration and Cadence

Cadence of interplay between player action and GM description.

NOTES:
 - ttrpgs are not *really* "collaborative storytelling". There is a deliberate asymmetry between players, who have majority of narrative agency but minimal of the creative control, and GM, who has the reverse.
 - There is a natural rhythm that mirrors three-beat storytelling structure with GM narration (setting), player action (tension), and GM response (resolution)
 - aim to give tools to GMs to match their narration style to the needs of the moment
 - the simple check for success is a microcosm of this!

### Narration and Mood

Two independent dimensions shape how a scene feels at the table: how much the **environment constrains movement**, and how much **time bears down on decisions**. These are often, but not always, related. They aren't rules, either, instead being features of the game itself that arise naturally through play. Understanding these axes helps the GM recognise when a scene is drifting in mood and decide whether to follow it or redirect it.

#### Axis : Spatial Restriction

At **low spatial restriction**, movement is trivially resolved. Players go where they want and geography is just flavour. The world is accessible and navigable, and the GM's attention is on what happens at locations rather than how to reach them. This produces an open, player-driven feel.

At **medium spatial restriction**, movement starts to become a strategic consideration. Not everywhere is reachable from anywhere. Paths have costs, and environmental factors come into play. Players must think about routes, resources, and the implications of where they are.

At **high spatial restriction**, positioning is critical. Movement becomes a limited resource that is granular and mechanically precise. 

The GM raises spatial restriction by introducing meaningful geographic stakes: distances that cost resources, paths that require decisions, locations that are genuinely hard to reach, or dangerous to be in. It drops in familiar territory, open roads, and safety.

#### Axis : Temporal Pressure

At **low temporal pressure**, the clock is generous. Events are ordered loosely, time passes in chunks of hours or days, and even retroactive actions are considered within reason. This produces an expansive, storybook feel.

At **medium temporal pressure**, the flow of time starts to become a mechanical component of play. The party must commit to their next move before something else happens, and resources deplete in meaningful intervals. Decisions become consequential without being frantic.

At **high temporal pressure**, every second is accounted for. The order of events is of critical importance.

The GM raises temporal pressure by introducing scenarios that threaten "failure states" if no action is taken. It drops when players are given room to deliberate, when time is summarized in chunks, and when consequences are no longer tied to in-game durations.

#### How the Axes Combine

These dimensions are closely related to each other and to the overall narrative tension of the game, but they can be varied independently, and different combinations reflect real modes of play.  The four game modes below represent the combinations this system has specific mechanics for, arranged from lowest to highest intensity.


### Game Modes

This system introduces specific modes of play; each has its own mechanics, and calls for a distinct narration style. The GM should announce mode changes clearly; once players become familiar with what these modes mean, this announcement will be enough to initiate the desired mood at the table.

#### Downtime

Downtime is **low spatial restriction** combined with **low temporal pressure**. Players are in a settlement or other safe, navigable location with time available and no immediate threat. Any known location is accessible without meaningful cost (but the GM should carefully consider how broadly "anywhere" should be applied). Time passes in hours, days, or longer.

Downtime should feel **expansive and player-driven**. Ask players what their characters want. Introduce the world through conversation, discovery, and the arrival of news. Not every moment needs a scene; summarize freely with scene cuts. Retroactive actions are also appropriate: if a character would obviously have done something during their two free days in town, they did it.

Downtime is the natural home of long-term consequences: healing, crafting, faction responses to the party's prior actions, rumors spreading, relationships developing. These are things that happen *over time* rather than *in a moment*.

*Example:* The party arrives in Mollen with four days before their contact returns. The GM gives each player one or two meaningful scenes: a late-night conversation at the inn; a suspicious figure watching the docks; a letter that arrives on day three; the rest is summarised or implied. There is little to no resource tracking. "Where do you each wake up on the fourth morning?"

#### Travel

Travel is **medium spatial restriction** combined with **low to medium temporal pressure**. Players are moving through wilderness or between regions. Movement follows connected geography, costs time and resources, and carries an ambient risk of environental danger or hostile encounters.

The GM narrates at the scale of several hours at a time. Each day's march or terrain feature crossed is a meaningful unit. Track what the situation calls for, such as supply, weather, terrain costs, or the occasional encounter, without simulating every hour.

*Example:* The party crosses the Greyfen Marshes. This is difficult terrain with no road, costing a full day. An encounter roll comes up empty, but the GM describes the sucking mud, a distant cry from something large, and the way the path seems to shift underfoot. That night, camping with poor shelter, they're short a ration. These accumulating pressures compose the texture of the journey.

Travel can tip toward medium temporal pressure when the party is being tracked, navigating in conditions that limit visibility, or racing to reach shelter before something closes in. Travel becomes Exploration the moment the party arrives at a specific location, and a higher degree of spatial resolution is required.

#### Exploration

Exploration is **high spatial restriction** combined with **medium temporal pressure**. Players are inside a dungeon, ruin, or other potentially hostile location. Every door, corridor, and decision point is potentially significant, and the threat of a hostile encounter is real. Movement is granular and deliberate and may even be tracked on a grid. Time passes in structured turns with tracked resources.

The GM shifts from narrator to **referee**. Describe what the senses register; don't volunteer what can't be perceived.

The dungeon turn is a chunk of time long enough to accomplish a single task, but short enough that players can make meaningful, reactive decisions balancing greed and fear: temporal pressure becomes a strategic component. "We can't spend another turn on this room."

*Example:* The party descends into the Vault of the Pale King. Torches are lit, and marching order is declared. The rogue advances and stops: she's heard something ahead. There's a patrol rounding a far corner. In Exploration mode, this timing of events and spatial decision to scout ahead matters in a way it wouldn't elsewhere.

Exploration becomes Pressure the moment a trap springs, a fight erupts, or time becomes critical in some other fashion.

#### Pressure

Pressure is **high spatial restriction** combined with **high temporal pressure**. Time is measured in seconds. Order of events is carefully controlled, and each action is committed the moment it's taken. The shift into Pressure should be **clear and deliberate**. The GM announces it, establishes the physical space if relevant (for example, with a grid map), and expects players to commit to actions in sequence.

The GM's job in Pressure is **clarity over atmosphere**. Players need accurate information about threats, terrain, and options. Don't hold back environmental details for dramatic effect; the situation already provides the drama.

*Example:* The party opens a door: they see a bound captive in a chair, a hooded figure with a crossbow, a burning fuse snaking toward a powder keg. The GM calls Pressure. Players must decide, right now, who does what and in what order. The rogue moves for the fuse. The fighter advances on the hooded figure. The mage is still crossing the threshold. These choices unfold in sequence and cannot be taken back.

**This is not only combat**. Resist letting combat be the only automatic trigger for Pressure.  It is any situation where the clock is an antagonist: a progressive trap, a ritual that must be interrupted, a negotiation where the wrong word ends the scene, water filling a chamber while the party searches for the exit, a public confrontation where a reputation is about to be destroyed, a chase through a collapsing building; all carry the same structure and drama.


## Core Modules

These are the core modules of the game implementing the modes of play.


### Overland Travel Ruleset

The Overland Travel ruleset governs movement across the wilderness in Travel mode. The world is represented as a hex map. Progress is measured in **legs** — half-day units of travel.

#### The Hex Map

Each hex represents a distinct terrain region. Terrain type determines the base movement cost to traverse that hex:

| Terrain  | Cost    |
|----------|---------|
| Plains   | 1 leg   |
| Forest   | 2 legs  |
| Hill     | 2 legs  |
| Desert   | 2 legs  |
| Tundra   | 2 legs  |
| Swamp    | 3 legs  |
| Mountain | 3 legs  |
| Jungle   | 3 legs  |
| Water    | —       |

Water hexes are impassable without a vessel. River travel by boat costs 1 leg per hex regardless of terrain.

**Roads** connect hex centres. A road reduces the cost of any hex it crosses by 1 leg (minimum 1).

**Rivers** run along hex edges. Crossing a river without a bridge or known ford costs +1 leg.

Under normal conditions the party can travel **2 legs per day**.

#### Points of Interest

Each hex is either **empty wilderness** or contains a single **Point of Interest (PoI)**. PoIs are one of three types:

- **Settlement** — a village, town, or city. Always visible from adjacent hexes.
- **Feature** — a neutral landmark: a ruin, a cave mouth, a standing stone, an unusual geography. May be hidden.
- **Dungeon** — a hostile location: a lair, a fortress, a cursed site. May be hidden.

A **hidden** PoI is not revealed until the party enters its hex or deliberately searches for it.

#### Visibility

The party always knows the **terrain type** of their current hex and all six adjacent hexes. Terrain beyond that range is unknown until visited or learned through other means (maps, rumour, scouts).

Visible, non-hidden PoIs in the seven-hex visible area are known to the party.

#### The Travel Turn

Each leg of travel follows this sequence:

1. **Declare destination.** The party chooses an adjacent hex to move into.
2. **Calculate cost.** Apply the terrain cost, then any road or river modifiers.
3. **Spend legs.** Deduct the cost from the day's travel budget. If the cost cannot be paid, the party must make camp or push on at resource cost.
4. **Encounter check.** The GM makes a wandering encounter roll appropriate to the region and terrain.
5. **Reveal.** Update the visible hex area. Reveal any non-hidden PoI in newly visible hexes.

### Pressure Scenario Ruleset

The Pressure Scenario ruleset governs mechanically intense, time-critical situations in Pressure mode: combat, countdown traps, chases, and any scenario where the timing and order of individual actions matters. When positioning is relevant to success or failure, Scenarios are typically played on a hex battlemap.

#### The Moment Clock

Time in a Pressure scenario is tracked in **moments**, each representing approximately one second of in-game time. The clock starts at **0** when the scenario begins and counts upward. The GM tracks the current moment, enviromental or NPC actions, and announces when a new moment begins. Each participant tracks their own **action moment**, the moment at which they may next take a major action. Players always have an opportunity to move and perform a minor action every moment.

#### Action Moments

At the start of a scenario, each participant rolls 2d4 to determine their first **action moment**, setting when they can first act. When a character takes a main action, they roll again and add the result to the current moment to set their **next action moment**.

The GM steps through moments in order, resolving each character's action when their moment arrives.

#### Movement

At the start of every moment, before resolving any actions, each character may move **one hex**. This movement is free; it does not consume an action and does not delay the action moment.

#### Actions

**Major actions** are a character's primary activity: main attacks and most character abilities, especially those that cost action points; anything that constitutes a complex task. A main action resolves at the character's action moment.

**Minor actions** are quick activities or instantaneous responses, especially those that come at no resource cost. Most characters have access to a standard list of minor actions in addition to a range of ability-specific ones. Taking a minor action pushes the character's next action moment forward by **1–3 moments**, depending on its complexity.

Standard minor actions include:
 - **Move (Cost 1)** Move an extra hex this moment.
 - **Drop/Stand (Cost 1/3)** Fall to/rise from prone
 - **Make Check (Cost 1)** Perform any skill check.

#### Action Points

Each time a character rolls their next action moment, their **action point pool** increases by half the total roll, rounded down. Depending on the circumstances and the player's abilities, other events may award action points too. Action points are the currency of most character abilities and major actions. The pool resets to 0 at the end of the scenario.

#### Ending the Scenario

The GM calls the end of Pressure mode when the immediate crisis resolves: the threat is neutralised, the escape is made, the negotiation concludes. Action point pools reset. The mode transitions to Exploration or Travel as appropriate to the situation.


## For Players

Player characters are defined by three **attributes** and a starting position in a shared **skill web**. Together these determine what a character is naturally capable of, how they approach problems, and what abilities they can develop.

### Attributes

Every character has three attributes that reflect their fundamental nature:

- **Might** — physical strength, toughness, and forceful action.
- **Wit** — perception, intelligence, and mental acuity.
- **Charm** — social grace, presence, and interpersonal influence.

At character creation, players distribute **3 points** across these three attributes. A value of 0 in an attribute represents a genuine weakness; 1 is competent; 2 is exceptional; 3 is a defining trait. All combinations are valid — a 3/0/0 specialist plays very differently from a 1/1/1 generalist.

### Skill Checks

When a character attempts something with an uncertain outcome, they make a **skill check**: roll **2d6** and add the most relevant attribute. The result is compared to a difficulty set by the GM.

When circumstances provide a meaningful advantage, roll **3d6 and drop the lowest**. When at a meaningful disadvantage, roll **3d6 and drop the highest**.

Skills in the skill web may modify checks further — granting flat bonuses, unlocking outcomes unavailable to the untrained, or allowing a different attribute to apply.

### Character Creation

Creating a character involves two decisions:

**1. Distribute attributes.** Allocate 3 points between Might, Wit, and Charm in any combination (including 0 in one or more).

**2. Choose three starting nodes in the skill web:**
- **Career** (Major node) — the character's primary professional identity and area of deepest expertise. This is their main entry point into the skill web; the skills they reach fastest cluster here. Examples: soldier, physician, scholar, thief, sailor.
- **Background** (Minor node) — who they were before, or alongside, their career: their origin, upbringing, or a prior life. Examples: farmhand, noble scion, street urchin, temple initiate.
- **Aspiration** (Minor node) — something the character is actively reaching toward: a goal, a discipline they've begun, a path they want to walk. Examples: duellist, naturalist, hedge mage, folk hero.

Each node grants the skills at that node and its immediate neighbours in the web, giving each starting cluster some immediate depth. Where clusters overlap, the reinforced skills are learned more thoroughly.

### The Skill Web

All player characters share a single **skill web** — a large directed graph of interconnected abilities. Each node is a skill, technique, or ability. Each directed edge is a prerequisite: you must hold the skill at one end before you can learn the skill at the other.

This system has no classes. Any character can reach any node in the web given time and development — but starting position determines how long that journey is. A career soldier reaches heavy armour techniques in a few steps; a scholar reaches the same node only after a long traverse through adjacent territory. Nothing is locked; everything is a matter of path.

In practice, the web is best understood as a collection of **thematic trees** — a cluster of martial skills, a cluster of arcane skills, a cluster of social skills, and so on — with connections running between them. These connections are the interesting design space: the crossover skills that sit between two traditions, accessible to characters who have developed in both directions.

As play progresses, characters unlock new skills by moving along edges from nodes they already hold. The GM may also open **new entry points** into the web — through factions joined, rare knowledge encountered, or significant events — granting access to nodes that would otherwise require a long path.

Modules and campaigns may attach their own sub-webs to the main graph, representing setting-specific traditions, faction abilities, or knowledge not available in the default world.
